Introduction

There are many types of pollution that affect the environment including land degradation, air pollution, water pollution and noise pollution. Among these, noise pollution is often overlooked as a serious environmental issue with greater attention typically being paid to other sources of pollution. However, exposure to excessive noise has been linked to a range of health issues such as hearing loss, sleep deprivation and elevated stress levels. For example, Ma et al. (2018) revealed that in China, many middle-aged individuals suffer from mental health disorders while some older adults report sleep disturbances due to noise exposure. Psychological effects can also include impaired cognitive functioning, discomfort and increased social isolation (Hemmat, 2023).

Noise pollution does not only affect humans, it can also disrupt wildlife ecosystems in ways that hinder social and reproductive activities (Seker et al.). Bats for instance, have shown reduced foraging and drinking behaviors when exposed to noise (Domer et al., 2021), and reef fish display behavioral changes in polluted coastal regions (Leduc et al., 2021). Even plant life can experience harmful ecological effects from noise as highlighted by Phillips et al. (2021). Influenced by social factors and personal disturbance, individuals or communities often respond to excessive noise by filing complaints (Zambon et al., 2020). Several studies have investigated variations in noise complaints, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. While Yildirim and Arefi (2021) reported a decrease in complaints during this period, others such as Ramphal et al. (2022) and Trudeau et al. (2023) noted an increase. Lee and Jeong (2021), for instance, found that noise complaints on social media, particularly Twitter, doubled during lockdowns compared to the previous year. Ramphal et al. (2022) investigated the pattern of noise complaints in New York between 2010 and 2021 and revealed that noise complaints were higher in low-income communities. These mixed findings suggest that patterns of noise complaints may be influenced by a range of contextual factors, including geographic location, socioeconomic status, policing activity, local incidents such as shootings and temporal dynamics.

As awareness of the harmful effects of noise pollution grows, so too has the number of complaints filed by residents. In 2024, noise complaints were the most common 311 non-emergency issue in New York City, with over 700,000 reports filed. On average, the city received more than 2,000 noise-related calls per day, underscoring its prominence as a public concern. While previous studies have examined noise complaints in relation to specific events such as the COVID-19 pandemic or focused on general temporal trends, few have systematically investigated how socioeconomic characteristics influence the spatial distribution of these complaints at the neighborhood level especially in New York City. This report addresses this gap by exploring the relationship between socioeconomic factors and the frequency of 311 noise complaints in New York City neighborhoods. The central research question guiding this study is: How are socioeconomic factors associated with the frequency and spatial distribution of noise complaints across NYC neighborhoods?

Literature Review

Several socio-economic indicators may influence the frequency and distribution of noise complaints. One of these socio-economic factors is the income level of residents in a community. For example, Ramphal et al (2022) using a linear mixed effects model to investigate the pattern of noise complaints in New York between 2010 and 2021 revealed that noise complaints were higher low-income communities. During the coronavirus pandemic which the study period covers, it must be noted that there was a rise in noise complaints in these lower-income communities.

Another study by Tong and Kang (2021) examine the impact of various socio-economic factors have on noise complaints in England. Using a correlation analysis, the study looked at how demographic factors, job related factors, property factors and deprivation factors influence the level of noise complaints. With regards to demographic factors, it was revealed that while population density has a positive relationship with noise complaints, age has a negative impact on noise complaints. In terms of job-related factors, the higher the number of unemployed people in an area, the higher the number of noise complaints. Additionally, in an area where the number of unemployed females is higher than unemployed males, noise complaints rise. However, there is the number of retirees and the level of noise complaints are negatively related. In relation to property factors, ownership of property such as cars or vans has a negative relationship with noise complaints. Deprivation factors examined how the access to or lack of basic amenities such as health, housing, income and employment affect the number of complaints. It was found out that the higher the level of deprivation of a city or area, the higher the number of noise complaints.

Exposure to noise may be responsible for the incidence of noise complaints based on socio-economic factors. In Montreal for instance, Dale et al (2015) found out more low-income households were exposed to the highest-level noise compared to their higher income counterparts. This indicates people of lower economic status may be more exposed to (high) noise. Casey et al (2017) confirms this assertion with the study provided evidence of the existence of racial inequities in noise exposure in the United States. Their study revealed that areas with more non-white residents recorded higher noise levels compared with less non-white residents. In the United States for instances, these non-white residents such as Hispanics, blacks and other working-class residents are exposed to transport noise from roads and aviation (Collins et al, 2020). This finding was the same for urban areas as well as suburban or rural areas. In contrast to evidence found in the United States and other similar studies, Zhang et al (2024) found out that wealthier communities are more exposed to higher noise levels compared with their more impoverished counterparts. This may be explained by rapid urbanization that is occurring in China.

Data and Methods

Data Aquisition

The 311 noise complaint data for 2022 and the latest New York City Neighborhood Tabulation Area (NTA) shapefiles were obtained from the NYC Open Data Portal NYC. The 311 dataset was cleaned by removing records with missing or invalid geographic coordinates, filtering for valid NYC latitude and longitude ranges, converting it to a spatial (sf) object and transforming its coordinate reference system to match the NTA shapefile. The Wakefield-Woodlawn neighborhood was excluded due to its outlier status with an unusually high volume of complaints from a single location. Additionally, American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates were sourced from the NYC Department of City Planning, joined to the NTA boundaries and later merged with the cleaned 311 data to facilitate socioeconomic and spatial correlation analysis.

Methods

After cleaning all datasets, assigning observations to Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (NTAs) and merging the data for analysis, an initial exploratory phase was conducted. This included mapping the spatial distribution of key socioeconomic variables such as age, gender, education, housing characteristics, poverty, income level and employment status. In parallel, the distribution of noise complaints across New York City was explored using bar charts, choropleth maps, and boxplots to highlight variation by neighborhood and borough. To investigate the study’s core questions, bivariate choropleth maps were employed to visually assess spatial associations between 311 noise complaint volumes and selected socioeconomic indicators.
